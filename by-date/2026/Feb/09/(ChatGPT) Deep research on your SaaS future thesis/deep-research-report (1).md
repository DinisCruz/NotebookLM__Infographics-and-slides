## Core thesis and how it differs from the “agents replace SaaS” narrative

Your memo argues for a middle path between two popular extremes: (a) “agents will replace most people and collapse software teams into one person with bots,” and (b) “GenAI will let every company vibe-code everything internally, so SaaS becomes obsolete.” Instead, you propose that GenAI shifts the *scaling function* of software: fewer people can build more, which expands the feasible set of tools that can exist, but it does **not** remove the economic value of “productisation” (reliability, security, governance, operations, support, compliance). ## What the data says about agentic development and individual leverage

The strongest near-term evidence for “one person can do more” comes from studies of AI coding assistants: controlled experiments and field trials repeatedly find meaningful speed or throughput gains, with variation by skill level and task type.

A widely cited controlled experiment on an engineering task (implementing an HTTP server) found that developers given access to an AI pair programmer completed the task **55.8% faster** than a control group. citeturn13search0

However, the more probative question for your thesis is: do these gains persist in real organisational settings, on real codebases, with real constraints? A large set of workplace RCTs across three enterprises reported that—while each individual experiment was noisy—pooling results across **4,867 developers** showed an estimated **26.08% increase** (standard error 10.3%) in completed tasks among developers using the tool, with larger gains and higher adoption among less experienced developers. citeturn14view0

## The productisation gap: why reliability, security, and governance become the differentiator

Your memo’s central mechanism—“anyone can prototype, but productisation is hard”—is strongly defensible, and it can be made concrete by breaking “productisation” into measurable disciplines.

Reliability engineering is one component. The SRE framing (service level objectives, error budgets, and written policies that trade off release velocity against reliability) exists precisely because mission-critical systems cannot be “mostly correct most of the time.” Reliability is treated as a managed product constraint, not just a technical detail. citeturn5search5turn5search1

The entity["organization","National Cyber Security Centre","uk cyber security agency"] has argued that prompt injection is not analogous to SQL injection because current LLMs do not enforce a robust boundary between “instructions” and “data” inside prompts. Similarly, the entity["organization","OWASP","web security nonprofit"] “Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications” catalogues common risks (prompt injection, insecure output handling, training data poisoning, and others) and treats them as a security engineering problem, not a temporary novelty. citeturn0search2turn0search12

## SaaS industry restructuring: from monoliths and rents to a “fat middle” of specialised vendors

Your “big SaaS companies will be shocked, and the mid-market of specialist vendors grows” thesis can be grounded in three interacting forces: lower build cost, higher switching feasibility, and demand for domain-specific “last-mile” fit.

Lower build cost is reasonably evidenced by developer tooling studies covered above: when tasks become 25–55% faster in many contexts, the addressable set of “economically buildable” applications expands, especially for narrow workflow problems. citeturn14view0turn13search0  The likely result is not “no SaaS,” but more SaaS categories and more entrants—particularly vertical and micro-SaaS products designed around specific functions, as you described with finance, budgeting, expenses, and procurement controls.

Switching feasibility is partly policy-driven, and this is where your Europe-centric optimism can be sharpened. Demand for domain-specific fit is a core part of your memo (“customers used to buy 100% features to get 10% of what they need”). While the exact “10%” number varies by context, the existence of SaaS waste and underutilisation is widely documented in practice, including public audits that estimate material costs from unused licences in large organisations. Your reference to “enshittification” (quality degradation driven by extraction incentives) adds a market-structure hypothesis: once customers have credible alternatives (via lower build cost, open-source options, and better switching), vendors that rely on lock-in and rent extraction lose pricing power unless they reinvest in customer value. This is where your open-source claim becomes more than ideology. Enterprise open-source adoption is widespread across core stack layers, and recent survey-based reporting shows meaningful penetration across operating systems, cloud/container tooling, web/app development, data management, and AI/ML. citeturn2search5  Meanwhile, public-sector and EU-level strategies explicitly encourage reuse and “Think Open” practices, signalling that (at least in Europe) procurement and governance can push toward open components and interoperability rather than closed suites. citeturn8search0turn8search4
